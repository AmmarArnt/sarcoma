## Part 2 — Liposomal Vitamin C: PK Reality Check [PATIENT TAKING]

**The marketing claim vs. the data.** Liposomal vitamin C is widely marketed as dramatically more bioavailable than "regular" ascorbic acid, sometimes with implied multiples in the range that would make oral dosing competitive with IV. The actual human PK literature supports a **real but modest** improvement, not a paradigm shift:

- A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study in 27 healthy adults given a single 500 mg dose found liposomal vitamin C produced **Cmax +27% and AUC0–24 +20%** versus non-liposomal ascorbic acid (Liposomal delivery enhances absorption of vitamin C into plasma and leukocytes, PMC11519160, 2024).
- A 2025 scoping review of 10 PK studies across different liposomal formulations, doses (0.15–10 g), and sampling durations found **9 of 10 studies showed higher bioavailability for liposomal vs non-liposomal ascorbate**, with reported ranges of **1.2–5.4-fold higher Cmax and 1.3–7.2-fold higher AUC** depending on formulation (Carr AC et al., "Do Liposomal Vitamin C Formulations Have Improved Bioavailability? A Scoping Review," *Basic Clin Pharmacol Toxicol* 2025, DOI via Wiley `10.1111/bcpt.70067`).
- Critically, **no PK study has shown a meaningful extension of plasma retention/elimination half-life** — liposomal formulations do not appear to change *how long* vitamin C stays in circulation, only the peak/area achieved from a given oral dose (same scoping review, 2025).

**Why this still doesn't reach "tumor-relevant" pharmacologic concentrations.** The oncology rationale for high-dose ascorbate (pro-oxidant, H2O2-generating effect preferentially toxic to cancer cells in vitro, Chen Q et al.) requires **plasma concentrations in the millimolar range (~1 mM and above)**. Oral vitamin C — by ANY formulation, including liposomal — is bounded by the body's tight homeostatic controls (intestinal absorption saturation, renal excretion) to a **ceiling around 200–250 µmol/L (~0.2–0.25 mM)** even at high doses (Levine M et al., NIH group; reviewed in "Intravenous High-Dose Vitamin C in Cancer Therapy," Frederick National Laboratory). A liposomal formulation that achieves, say, a 2–5× Cmax improvement over plain ascorbate at this oral ceiling is still moving within roughly the **same one-log-unit band below the ~1 mM pharmacologic threshold**, not crossing into it.

- **IV pharmacologic ascorbate** (the clinically-studied route, e.g., NCT03508726 — high-dose ascorbate with preoperative radiation in locally advanced soft-tissue sarcomas, and multiple other solid-tumor trials) bypasses gut/renal controls entirely and reaches **~25–30 mM plasma concentrations safely with 100 g IV infusions** (Levine group phase I data; Clinical experience with IV ascorbic acid, PMC3751545).
- **Order-of-magnitude gap**: oral/liposomal ceiling (~0.2 mM) vs. IV pharmacologic levels (~25–30 mM) is roughly a **100–150× gap**. Liposomal encapsulation's documented 1.2–5.4× improvement over plain oral ascorbate does not close a 100×+ gap.

**Bottom line for this patient**: liposomal vitamin C is a real, modestly-better-absorbed form of oral vitamin C — useful for correcting any dietary insufficiency — but it does **not** approximate the IV pharmacologic-ascorbate exposure that is the basis of the oncology trial literature (including the soft-tissue-sarcoma trial above). Framing it as "doing what IV high-dose vitamin C does, just orally" is not supported by the PK data. **Evidence tier: Mechanistic** for any V1/V2 antioxidant-axis contribution at oral/liposomal exposures; **Clinical-Trial** tier applies only to the IV route, which is a different intervention entirely.

**Tissue distribution**: Vitamin C does concentrate in some tissues above plasma (e.g., adrenal gland, leukocytes — the 2024 RCT above measured leukocyte ascorbate as well as plasma, finding liposomal delivery also enhanced leukocyte loading). Tumor-tissue ascorbate concentration in CIC-DUX4 or any soft-tissue sarcoma at oral/liposomal dosing is **unestablished**.

## Part 3 — Thymoquinone / Black Cumin Seed Oil (*Nigella sativa*): Bioavailability Profile [PATIENT TAKING]

**Is there human PK data at all? Essentially no — and this needs to be said plainly.** A 2023 study developing a GC-MS method for thymoquinone detection in oil and serum for clinical PK studies reports that when healthy volunteers ingested **highly concentrated *Nigella sativa* oil, thymoquinone and/or its derivatives were NOT detectable in human serum after oral intake** — despite the assay being validated to detect spiked thymoquinone at 5–10 µg/mL (GC-MS Detection of Thymoquinone in Oil and Serum for Clinical PK Studies, PMC10671713). This is the closest thing to a direct human oral-PK data point that exists, and **the result is a non-detect**.

- **Solubility**: Thymoquinone is a lipophilic monoterpene ketone (log P favorable for lipid-phase partitioning). Being delivered as an oil (black cumin seed oil) is itself a reasonable lipid-based delivery vehicle — co-administration with additional dietary fat is mechanistically plausible to aid micellarization and lymphatic uptake, but **no human study has quantified this effect** for thymoquinone specifically `[no direct citation; mechanism inferred from general lipophilic-compound absorption principles]`.
- **Animal PK exists but does not transfer directly**: rabbit IV/oral PK studies report measurable plasma thymoquinone (clearance ~7.2 mL/kg/min, Vss ~700 mL/kg; PMID 24924310), and rat studies show gender-dependent Cmax (4.5–5.2 µg/mL) with rapid elimination after oral dosing (ScienceDirect, *Pharmacokinetic gender effect... in male and female rats*, 2020). These confirm thymoquinone IS absorbed and distributed in animals at the doses used — but animal doses in these PK studies are typically far above realistic black-cumin-seed-oil culinary/supplemental intake in humans, and the human non-detect result above suggests the gap may be substantial.
- **Net assessment**: **Confidence: Low.** We cannot state an achievable human plasma thymoquinone concentration with any reliability — the one human oral study identified found it below detection limits. Any V1/V3 mechanistic claim for thymoquinone (e.g., NF-κB modulation, weak HDAC-related effects cited in the cell-line literature) should be tagged **Theoretical-at-achievable-exposure** even though the underlying cell-line mechanism itself is Preclinical-Cell.

**Tissue distribution**: No human tumor-tissue data exist (consistent with no human plasma data existing). **Unestablished.**

**Chemo-interaction note (carried forward regardless of the PK uncertainty)**: thymoquinone has documented in vitro CYP3A4-inhibitory activity in the preclinical literature. This interaction concern does **not** depend on resolving the human plasma-PK question — even a compound that is poorly absorbed systemically can still exert a CYP3A4 effect at the gut-wall/first-pass level, which is where much of the ifosfamide-prodrug-activation and vincristine/etoposide-metabolism concern arises. **This is the same CYP3A4/P-gp axis flagged for piperine and curcumin** (see Part 5) — the patient is taking three CYP3A4-relevant compounds simultaneously (piperine, curcumin, thymoquinone) heading into high-dose ifosfamide.

## Part 4 — Curcumin + Piperine [PATIENT TAKING] — Mandatory Shoba 1998 Entry

**Solubility / meal timing**: Curcumin is highly lipophilic (log P ~3.3) and essentially insoluble in water — it must be taken with dietary fat for any meaningful absorption. If the patient's curcumin+piperine supplement is taken without a fat-containing meal, absorption is likely negligible regardless of the piperine.

**First-pass metabolism**: Extensive. Intestinal glucuronidation (UGT1A8, UGT1A10) and sulfation (SULT1A1), plus hepatic first-pass, dominate. In most published PK studies of unformulated curcumin, **free plasma curcumin is at or below the assay's limit of detection**.

**The Shoba et al. 1998 caveat — reproduced in full (mandatory):**

> The widely-cited "~2000% bioavailability increase" for curcumin + piperine comes from Shoba G, Joy D, Joseph T, Majeed M, Rajendran R, Srinivas PS, "Influence of piperine on the pharmacokinetics of curcumin in animals and human volunteers," *Planta Medica*, 1998. This was a **single-dose pharmacokinetic study in n=10 healthy human volunteers**, comparing 2 g curcumin alone vs. 2 g curcumin + 20 mg piperine. **The curcumin-only control arm produced serum curcumin levels below the assay's limit of detection.** Because the denominator (curcumin-alone plasma level) was effectively zero/below-LOD, the "2000%" (or commonly stated "20×") figure is computed as a ratio against a near-zero baseline — it is not a stable, generalizable fold-change that can be expected to reproduce across formulations, doses, or populations. **The directional finding — that co-administered piperine increases curcumin absorption — is real and has been reproduced in later studies.** But **the specific 2000% / 20× multiplier must NOT be cited as a universal bioavailability-enhancement factor.** Any claim of "piperine gives you 20× more curcumin absorption" stated as a general rule misrepresents this single, small, single-dose study.

**What the evidence actually supports beyond Shoba 1998**: Even in the Shoba 1998 curcumin+piperine arm, the detectable serum curcumin level was on the order of low-µg/mL (commonly cited as ~1.8 µg/mL, including conjugated metabolites, ≈ 4.9 µM total curcuminoid-equivalent). For comparison, the V1-B mechanism (BRD4-chromatin disruption / BRD4 BD1 binding reported in cell-line studies) typically operates at **5–20 µM of FREE (unconjugated) curcumin** — and the circulating species in human PK studies are predominantly glucuronide/sulfate conjugates, not free curcumin. So even the best-case Shoba 1998 arm sits at or below the lower bound of the cell-line active range, and that's before accounting for the conjugate-vs-free-compound activity question.

**PK-enhancing combinations with documented effect sizes:**

| Formulation | Enhancement vs. unformulated curcumin | Citation | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Piperine (20 mg with 2 g curcumin) | Directional increase; "2000%"/"20×" computed against a below-LOD control | Shoba G et al., *Planta Medica* 1998 | n=10, single dose, healthy volunteers — see full caveat above. Do not cite as universal multiplier. |
| Phospholipid complex (Meriva) | ~29× higher AUC vs. unformulated curcumin | Cuomo J et al., *J Nat Prod* 2011, PMID 21413822 | Crossover PK in healthy volunteers; not tested in sarcoma patients. |

**CRITICAL chemo-interaction note**: Piperine's PK-enhancing effect on curcumin operates substantially through **CYP3A4 inhibition and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibition** — the very mechanisms that increase systemic exposure to curcumin also slow ifosfamide's hepatic activation pathway and alter vincristine/etoposide clearance (both P-gp substrates). **The PK benefit and the chemo-interaction risk are mechanistically the same phenomenon — they cannot be separated.** This is the central finding for the lead's reconciliation regarding this compound pair.

**Tissue distribution to mesenchymal tumor**: No published data for CIC-DUX4 or any soft-tissue sarcoma. Rodent studies show curcumin distribution to liver and intestinal mucosa, with some evidence of pulmonary accumulation — relevant in principle to this patient's lung oligometastasis, but **no human quantitative data exist**, and a post-radiation lung (whole-lung irradiation was part of this patient's treatment history) has altered vasculature/tissue architecture that further limits extrapolation from normal-lung rodent data. **Unestablished.**

### Sulforaphane: Juicing vs. Chewing/Chopping — The Critical Preparation Finding

Sulforaphane does not exist as such in intact broccoli — its precursor **glucoraphanin** is converted to sulforaphane only when **myrosinase** (a thioglucosidase co-localized in separate plant cell compartments) contacts glucoraphanin after **cell disruption** (chopping, chewing, crushing). This reaction:

1. Requires physical disruption of plant cell structure.
2. Requires active myrosinase — the enzyme is **heat-labile** (inactivated around 70°C) and **shear-sensitive**.
3. Takes approximately **40 minutes at room temperature** to reach substantial conversion when broccoli/sprouts are chopped and allowed to stand.

**Centrifugal juicing is mechanistically positioned to defeat this activation pathway**: the high-speed blade generates shear forces and localized frictional heat that can denature myrosinase before or during the brief contact window with glucoraphanin, and the rapid processing time does not allow the ~40-minute room-temperature reaction to proceed before the juice is consumed (typically immediately). Without active myrosinase, the **gut microbiome** performs some glucoraphanin-to-sulforaphane conversion, but this route is estimated to yield **substantially less sulforaphane** than myrosinase-mediated conversion — published comparisons describe several-fold-lower yields from heat-treated (myrosinase-inactivated) broccoli relative to fresh/raw, myrosinase-active preparations (Vermeulen M et al., *J Nutr* 2008, PMID 18539765, in the context of cooked vs. raw broccoli — directly analogous to the juicing question even though juicing itself was not the variable studied).

**Net effect for this patient**: the broccoli component of the rest-week juice is **very likely delivering near-zero bioactive sulforaphane** under the patient's current preparation method (broccoli juiced together with celery, ginger, carrot, apple, beetroot in a centrifugal juicer). Achievable plasma sulforaphane is probably **<0.05–0.2 µM** (gut-bacterial-conversion-only route) rather than the **0.5–2 µM** achievable with proper myrosinase activation — moving sulforaphane from "best concentration ratio in V1" (3–10× mismatch) to "one of the worst" (>25–100× mismatch), purely as a function of preparation method, with **no change needed to the ingredient itself**.

A corrective preparation exists and has supporting evidence: chopping/crushing broccoli or broccoli sprouts and letting them stand ~40 minutes at room temperature before consumption preserves myrosinase activity; alternatively, adding a small amount of a separate myrosinase-rich source (e.g., daikon radish, mustard seed powder) to heat- or shear-processed broccoli has been shown to rescue sulforaphane formation in human studies (Vermeulen M et al., *J Nutr* 2008, PMID 18539765). **This is a preparation-method observation, not a dosing recommendation** — no specific quantity is being prescribed.

## Forward Hypotheses

**[Forward Hypothesis 1]** — Gut-luminal / pre-absorptive exposure may be mechanistically relevant even when plasma concentrations are sub-threshold. Nearly all of the concentration-mismatch analysis above concerns *systemic plasma* exposure. But polyphenols, isothiocyanates, and thymoquinone pass through the gastrointestinal lumen at concentrations many-fold higher than their eventual plasma levels (the full ingested dose, undiluted by systemic distribution, contacts the gut epithelium and gut-resident immune cells directly). If any V1-relevant mechanism (e.g., RAS/ERK modulation, weak HDAC activity) operates on gut-associated lymphoid tissue, gut-resident immune cell populations, or the gut microbiome's metabolic output (short-chain fatty acids, secondary bile acids) rather than requiring direct systemic delivery to the tumor, a "concentration mismatch" framed purely in plasma terms could understate the biologically relevant exposure. **Mechanistic basis**: gut-lumen concentrations of ingested phytochemicals are routinely 10–100× higher than peak plasma concentrations for the same dose, because only a fraction is absorbed. **What would test it**: an ex vivo or in vivo (mouse) study comparing gut-luminal vs. plasma concentrations of sulforaphane/curcumin/thymoquinone after an equivalent juice-regimen dose, paired with a readout of gut-immune-cell activation markers (rather than tumor-cell BRD4/CDK4 readouts) — this would reframe the relevant V1/V4-adjacent question as "does this regimen condition gut immunity" rather than "does this regimen directly modulate the tumor cell."

**[Forward Hypothesis 2]** — Repeated sub-threshold dosing over months (as in this patient's year of NED self-administration) could produce a cumulative pharmacodynamic effect not captured by single-dose PK studies. All the PK data cited above (Shoba 1998, Cuomo 2011, the liposomal vitamin C trials, the apigenin/celery studies) are **single-dose or short-duration** studies measuring peak plasma concentrations. They cannot rule out that daily, sustained, low-level exposure over months produces adaptive changes (e.g., altered phase-II enzyme expression, sustained low-grade Nrf2 pathway activation from sulforaphane/curcumin even at sub-µM levels, or epigenetic drift from sustained low-dose polyphenol exposure) that a single-dose Cmax/AUC study would not detect. **Mechanistic basis**: Nrf2-ARE pathway activation by isothiocyanates and polyphenols is reported to occur at lower concentrations than direct BRD4/CDK4 binding assays, and chronic low-dose exposure paradigms in the chemoprevention literature sometimes show effects not predicted from acute PK. **What would test it**: a repeated-dosing PK/PD study (weeks, not single-dose) measuring a sensitive pharmacodynamic biomarker (e.g., Nrf2-target gene expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, or urinary phase-II metabolite ratios) rather than parent-compound plasma Cmax, in volunteers consuming a juice regimen analogous to this patient's over 4–8 weeks. This would not establish a CIC-DUX4-specific effect but would test whether the "concentration mismatch" conclusion changes under chronic dosing — and would be a relatively low-cost human study to design.

## What I Could Not Establish

1. **CIC-DUX4 (or any soft-tissue sarcoma) tumor-tissue concentration data for any compound in this output.** This does not exist in the published literature, for any compound, at any dose. Plasma-to-tumor extrapolation throughout this output is an assumption.

2. **The actual sulforaphane yield from this patient's specific juicer.** Centrifugal juicers are mechanistically likely to destroy myrosinase, but cold-press/masticating juicer models may preserve more enzyme activity — this has not been characterized in published literature for any juicer type, and I could not find a study directly comparing juicer mechanisms for myrosinase preservation.

3. **Whether circulating phase-II conjugates (glucuronides/sulfates) of quercetin, EGCG, apigenin, and curcumin retain meaningful activity at the V1-B/A mechanism targets.** This is the single largest unresolved question for translating ANY of this output's plasma-concentration data into a mechanism claim — cell-line studies almost always use the parent (unconjugated) compound, but the parent compound is rarely what circulates in human plasma after oral intake.

4. **A confirmed human plasma thymoquinone concentration at any dose.** The one study identified found non-detectable levels after oral *Nigella sativa* oil in healthy volunteers. I could not find a study reporting a positive human plasma thymoquinone value.

5. **Cumulative/interactive CYP3A4 inhibition from piperine + curcumin + thymoquinone taken together**, in the specific context of a patient about to receive high-dose ifosfamide. Each compound's CYP3A4-inhibitory activity is documented individually (mostly in vitro); a three-way interaction study in cancer patients on this drug combination does not exist.

6. **Whether the apigenin/luteolin plasma data from parsley/celery-leaf studies generalize to celery STALK juice** (the form this patient consumes) — the cited studies used parsley or celery leaf preparations, which may differ in apigenin/luteolin content from stalk juice.
